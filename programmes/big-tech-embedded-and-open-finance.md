## Aim and Purpose

To give supervisors a clear understanding of how large technology platforms, embedded finance providers and open finance ecosystems deliver financial services, and where accountability and risk sit along the value chain. Participants leave able to map platform and partnership models, challenge regulated firms and their partners, and contribute to the authority's approach to the perimeter, data access and cross-authority cooperation.

**Coverage:** Platform business models in finance, embedded finance and banking-as-a-service, data access and open finance, activity-based and entity-based regulation, concentration, competition and financial stability, operational dependency on platforms, consumer outcomes in digital journeys, and supervising platforms and partnerships in practice.

## Intended Audience

- Supervisors of banks, payment and e-money institutions, lenders and insurers that partner with platforms or distribute through them
- Policy, innovation and authorisation staff dealing with new entrants, perimeter questions and open finance
- Senior managers coordinating with competition, data protection and consumer authorities

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain how big tech platforms and embedded finance providers enter and operate in financial services
- Assess banking-as-a-service and distribution partnerships, including accountability for outsourced customer relationships
- Evaluate open finance data-sharing frameworks and the risks of data concentration
- Analyse the case for activity-based and entity-based approaches to regulating platforms
- Identify concentration, operational dependency and consumer harm risks arising from platform finance
- Design a supervisory approach to platforms and partnerships, including cooperation with other authorities

## Syllabus

### Module 1: Platform Business Models in Finance

How large technology platforms enter financial services, the data, network and activity loop that drives their advantage, and how their models differ from traditional financial firms.

**Objectives**

- Explain the economics of platforms and the data-network-activities loop
- Distinguish the routes by which big tech enters payments, credit, insurance and wealth
- Identify where platform models create new supervisory challenges

**Syllabus**

- Network effects, ecosystems and cross-subsidisation
- Entry routes: own licence, partnership or acquisition
- Platform finance in advanced and emerging economies
- BIS and FSB analysis of big tech in finance

### Module 2: Embedded Finance and Banking-as-a-Service

How financial products are embedded into non-financial journeys and delivered through banking-as-a-service partnerships, and where regulatory responsibility sits when the customer relationship is outsourced.

**Objectives**

- Map the roles of licensed providers, platforms and intermediaries in embedded finance
- Assess the sponsor firm's oversight of partners and programme managers
- Evaluate red flags in fast-growing banking-as-a-service arrangements

**Syllabus**

- Embedded payments, lending, insurance and investment
- Banking-as-a-service: sponsor banks, programme managers and middleware
- Partner onboarding, AML/CFT controls and reconciliation of client funds
- Case studies: partnership failures and supervisory findings

### Module 3: Data Access and Open Finance

How open banking and open finance frameworks enable customer-permissioned data sharing, the design choices behind them, and the competition, privacy and security trade-offs supervisors need to understand.

**Objectives**

- Explain the design of open banking and open finance frameworks
- Assess consent, liability and security arrangements for data sharing
- Evaluate the risk that data access entrenches rather than reduces platform power

**Syllabus**

- Open banking to open finance: scope and data categories
- APIs, standards, consent dashboards and liability models
- Data protection, fraud and cybersecurity risks in data sharing
- Reciprocity and data flows between big tech and incumbents

### Module 4: Activity-Based and Entity-Based Regulation

Why the perimeter struggles with platforms that combine regulated and unregulated activities, and the arguments for activity-based rules, entity-based requirements or a combination of the two.

**Objectives**

- Compare activity-based and entity-based approaches to regulating platforms
- Analyse perimeter gaps created by unregulated activities within platform groups
- Evaluate group-level and holding company options for large platforms

**Syllabus**

- Same activity, same risk, same regulation: strengths and limits
- Entity-based requirements for systemically relevant platforms
- Mixed-activity groups and intragroup dependencies
- FSB and BIS work on regulatory approaches to big tech

### Module 5: Concentration, Competition and Financial Stability

How platform scale, data advantages and partnership networks can concentrate risk and market power, and when platform finance becomes a financial stability concern.

**Objectives**

- Analyse concentration risks in distribution, data and infrastructure
- Assess the interaction between competition and financial stability objectives
- Identify indicators that a platform has become systemically relevant

**Syllabus**

- Market power, lock-in, self-preferencing and data advantages
- Rapid deposit and credit flows through platform channels
- Interconnections with banks, funds and payment systems
- Coordination with competition authorities

### Module 6: Operational Dependency and Third-Party Risk

How financial firms' reliance on platforms for distribution, cloud, data and customer interfaces creates operational dependency, and how supervisors assess resilience and exit options.

**Objectives**

- Assess firms' operational dependency on platform partners and providers
- Evaluate contractual rights, oversight and exit planning for critical platform relationships
- Apply operational resilience principles to embedded and platform-based services

**Syllabus**

- Distribution dependency and loss of the customer interface
- Cloud, data and technology dependencies on platform groups
- Contractual rights, audit access and substitutability
- Links to critical third-party oversight regimes

### Module 7: Consumer Outcomes on Digital Platforms

How platform design, personalisation and data use shape consumer decisions, the risks of harm and exclusion, and the evidence supervisors should seek on outcomes in embedded journeys.

**Objectives**

- Identify consumer harms from choice architecture, dark patterns and frictionless credit
- Assess disclosure, suitability and complaints handling in embedded journeys
- Evaluate the financial inclusion benefits and risks of platform finance

**Syllabus**

- Choice architecture, defaults and dark patterns
- Embedded credit and buy now, pay later products
- Personalised pricing, profiling and fair treatment
- Who owns the complaint: redress across the partnership chain

### Module 8: Supervising Platforms and Partnerships in Practice

Brings the programme together into a practical supervisory approach to platforms and partnerships, from data and early warning indicators to cooperation with other authorities and the authority's policy stance.

**Objectives**

- Apply a supervisory assessment framework to a platform partnership
- Evaluate cooperation arrangements with competition, data protection and consumer authorities
- Design priorities for the authority's approach to platform finance

**Syllabus**

- Mapping partnership chains and accountability
- Early warning indicators and thematic reviews of partnerships
- Cross-authority and cross-border cooperation
- Workshop: supervisory review of an embedded finance partnership

## Structure and Format

Eight modules of about three hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a multi-day programme or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a briefing with case studies and supervisory question exercises, closing with a partnership review workshop, and modules can be selected or tailored to the authority's needs.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some experience of banking, payments or conduct supervision is helpful. No technical background is required.
