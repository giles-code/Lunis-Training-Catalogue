## Aim and Purpose

To give directors a practical, jargon-free grounding in how AI is used in financial services and the risks it brings, with the governance tools to oversee it. Participants leave able to set the board's expectations, challenge management and the control functions, and evidence effective oversight to regulators and auditors.

**Coverage:** AI strategy and risk appetite, governance and accountability, model risk and explainability, fairness and customer outcomes, AI in trading and advice, resilience and third-party risk, AI regulation across jurisdictions, board reporting and assurance, and the board's own capability.

## Intended Audience

- Chairs, non-executive and independent directors and board committee members, especially on risk, audit and technology committees
- Executive directors and senior managers accountable for AI, risk and compliance
- Company secretaries and heads of governance supporting board oversight

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain in plain language how AI works, where the firm uses it and what can go wrong
- Define the board's AI accountabilities and evaluate the firm's governance framework
- Challenge AI strategy and set a risk appetite with measurable limits
- Oversee model risk, fairness and customer outcomes using structured board-level questions
- Assess resilience, third-party and regulatory risk, including emerging AI regulation
- Specify the information and assurance the board needs, and agree an action plan

## Syllabus

### Module 1: Artificial Intelligence for Directors

Plain-language grounding in how machine learning and generative AI are built and deployed, how they differ from rules-based tools, and why they change the firm's risk profile.

**Objectives**

- Explain how AI systems learn, decide and fail
- Distinguish machine learning, generative AI and traditional models, and their risks
- Identify where AI is already embedded in the firm, including through vendors

**Syllabus**

- How AI works: data, training, testing and deployment
- Machine learning versus generative AI and large language models
- Build versus buy: accountability for third-party AI
- The vocabulary directors need: drift, bias, hallucination, explainability

### Module 2: Board Accountability and AI Governance Structures

Where accountability for AI sits, what a sound governance framework looks like, and how oversight is divided between the board, its committees and management.

**Objectives**

- Define the board's responsibilities for AI and distinguish them from management's
- Evaluate the firm's AI governance framework, policies and model inventory
- Allocate oversight across committees and the three lines of defence

**Syllabus**

- Directors' duties and senior executive accountability for AI-driven decisions
- Elements of a governance framework: policy, inventory, ownership and escalation
- Committee mandates: board, risk, audit and technology
- Common governance failures and their consequences

### Module 3: AI Strategy, Risk Appetite and Value

How boards decide where AI should and should not be used, test management's business cases, and set a risk appetite that balances innovation against conduct, prudential and operational risk.

**Objectives**

- Challenge management's AI strategy and claimed benefits
- Set an AI risk appetite with measurable limits and escalation triggers
- Weigh innovation against conduct, prudential and operational risk

**Syllabus**

- Questions to ask of an AI strategy: purpose, benefit, cost, dependency and exit
- Drafting a risk appetite statement: in-scope uses, prohibited uses and limits
- Avoiding hype and paralysis: pilots and staged adoption
- Investment, capability and talent decisions for board approval

### Module 4: Model Risk, Explainability and Board Challenge

How to oversee model risk for machine learning and generative AI, what explainability can realistically deliver, and the questions the board should put to management and model validators.

**Objectives**

- Explain why complex and generative models carry greater model risk
- Assess model documentation, validation and monitoring standards
- Use a structured question set to probe model risk assurance

**Syllabus**

- Opacity, drift and over-reliance in complex models
- Explainability by use case, from credit decisions to trading
- Good practice in documentation, independent validation and monitoring
- A board question set for approving, extending or retiring a model

### Module 5: Fairness, Conduct and Consumer Outcomes

The board's responsibility for fair outcomes where AI makes or influences decisions: how bias arises, how fairness is tested, and how the board gains assurance on customer treatment.

**Objectives**

- Explain how bias and proxy discrimination enter AI-driven decisions
- Evaluate whether bias testing and outcome monitoring give real assurance
- Identify the conduct, legal and reputational exposure of unfair outcomes

**Syllabus**

- Sources of bias: historical data, proxies and feedback loops
- Fairness measures in credit, insurance pricing and targeting
- Consumer protection, fair treatment and non-discrimination obligations applied to AI
- Case studies of AI bias harms and remediation

### Module 6: AI in Trading, Advice and Client Services

The business-line uses of AI with the greatest conduct and market integrity risk, and the controls, kill-switches and escalation arrangements the board should expect.

**Objectives**

- Explain how AI is used in trading, investment and client-facing advice
- Assess market integrity, suitability and mis-selling risks
- Judge the adequacy of controls, human oversight and escalation protocols

**Syllabus**

- Algorithmic trading, portfolio construction and signal generation
- Market integrity risks: correlated behaviour and flash events
- Robo-advice, chatbots and the guidance versus advice boundary
- Kill-switches and escalation: what the board should require

### Module 7: Operational Resilience, Cyber and Third-Party AI Risk

How AI systems fail and can be attacked, how reliance on a few AI vendors concentrates risk, and whether the firm can withstand and recover from an AI failure.

**Objectives**

- Recognise how AI fails, including drift, data poisoning and prompt injection
- Assess concentration and dependency on third-party AI providers
- Evaluate resilience testing and incident escalation to the board

**Syllabus**

- Drift, silent degradation and late detection of AI problems
- Adversarial attacks and the board's cyber oversight role
- Vendor and foundation-model dependency: due diligence and exit planning
- Operational resilience and outsourcing expectations applied to AI

### Module 8: AI Regulation and Regulatory Expectations of Boards

The legal and regulatory obligations AI creates for the firm and the board: how jurisdictions are regulating AI, how this overlays existing financial services rules, and what supervisors and auditors expect to see.

**Objectives**

- Explain the main approaches to AI regulation and the duties they place on firms
- Identify which of the firm's AI uses are likely to be treated as high-risk and the assurance they require
- Prepare the board to evidence oversight to supervisors and auditors

**Syllabus**

- Approaches to AI regulation across jurisdictions: risk-based laws, sectoral rules, supervisory guidance and managing divergence
- International standards and frameworks, including OECD, NIST, ISO/IEC 42001, IOSCO, FSB and BIS
- Interaction with existing conduct, prudential, consumer protection and data protection rules
- Preparing for supervisory engagement: what the board should be able to show

### Module 9: Board Reporting, Management Information and Assurance

The information boards need to oversee AI, how to read dashboards and incident reports critically, and how to obtain credible independent assurance rather than relying on assertion.

**Objectives**

- Specify the management information and indicators the board needs
- Interpret AI reports and identify what is missing
- Obtain independent assurance from internal audit and external reviewers

**Syllabus**

- The core board information set: inventory, risk ratings, incidents and outcomes
- Key risk indicators and early warning measures
- Recognising comfort without evidence
- Internal audit, external validation and regulatory reviews as assurance

### Module 10: Leading AI Oversight: Capability, Culture and Emerging Risks

Brings the programme together into a board action plan covering the board's own capability, culture, emerging risks from generative AI and agents, and keeping governance current.

**Objectives**

- Assess the board's AI literacy and plan to close gaps
- Anticipate governance issues from generative AI and autonomous agents
- Produce a board action plan for AI oversight

**Syllabus**

- Board skills, external advice and how much expertise is enough
- Culture and speaking up on AI concerns
- Generative AI and autonomous agents: accountability and control questions
- Workshop: the board's twelve-month AI oversight plan

## Structure and Format

Ten modules of about two hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a two- to three-day programme or as a series of board briefing sessions. Each module combines a short briefing with case studies and a board question set, and modules can be selected or tailored to the board's needs.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Board or senior management experience in financial services is helpful. No technical background in AI or data science is required.

## Core Reading

- OECD, Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence (2019, revised 2024)
- NIST, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) (2023)
- ISO/IEC 42001:2023, Artificial Intelligence – Management System
- FSB, The Financial Stability Implications of Artificial Intelligence (2024)
