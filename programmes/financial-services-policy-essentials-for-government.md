## Aim and Purpose

To give officials in finance ministries, treasuries and other public bodies a practical, non-technical grounding in the financial system, the architecture of financial regulation and the respective roles of government and independent regulators. Participants leave able to frame financial services policy, ask the right questions of regulators and industry, and represent the national interest in international standard-setting and peer review.

**Coverage:** How the financial system works, the economic case for regulation, regulatory architecture and models, independence and accountability of regulators, international standards and peer reviews, banking, insurance, securities and payments, financial stability, financial crime, the policy levers available to government, and designing a national financial services policy.

## Intended Audience

- Officials in finance ministries and treasuries responsible for financial services, banking and capital markets policy
- Senior officials and advisers to ministers who oversee or appoint financial regulators
- Staff of public agencies, cabinet offices and parliamentary committees who work on financial sector issues

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain how the financial system channels savings into investment and where it can fail
- Compare the main models of regulatory architecture and their strengths and weaknesses
- Distinguish the roles of government and independent regulators and design effective accountability
- Explain the international standards and peer review processes that apply to the national system
- Identify the policy levers available to government and the trade-offs between them
- Design the elements of a coherent national financial services policy

## Syllabus

### Module 1: How the Financial System Works

The core functions of the financial system, the main institutions and markets that perform them, and how money, credit and risk flow through the economy.

**Objectives**

- Explain the economic functions of banks, insurers, markets and payment systems
- Describe how institutions and markets connect households, firms and government
- Identify the links between the financial sector, public finances and growth

**Syllabus**

- Intermediation, maturity transformation, risk transfer and payments
- Banks, insurers, pension schemes, funds and non-bank lenders
- Money, bond, equity and derivatives markets
- The financial sector's contribution to growth, tax and employment

### Module 2: Why Regulate: Market Failures and Policy Objectives

The economic case for financial regulation, the objectives regulators pursue, and the tensions between stability, consumer protection, market integrity, competition and growth.

**Objectives**

- Explain the market failures that justify financial regulation
- Distinguish prudential, conduct, market integrity and financial crime objectives
- Assess the trade-offs between regulatory objectives and economic growth

**Syllabus**

- Information asymmetry, externalities, moral hazard and contagion
- Statutory objectives and how they are framed in legislation
- Competition and growth objectives: benefits and risks
- Lessons from crises on the cost of regulatory failure

### Module 3: Regulatory Architecture: Models and Choices

How countries organise financial regulation, from sectoral and integrated regulators to twin peaks models, and the role of central banks, ministries and other public bodies.

**Objectives**

- Compare sectoral, integrated, twin peaks and hybrid models
- Explain the role of the central bank in prudential and macroprudential policy
- Evaluate coordination arrangements between authorities

**Syllabus**

- Sectoral, integrated and twin peaks models compared
- Central banks, financial stability committees and deposit insurers
- Memoranda of understanding and inter-agency coordination
- Case studies of architecture reform after a crisis

### Module 4: Independence and Accountability of Regulators

Why regulators are made operationally independent, the safeguards that protect independence, and how government and legislators hold regulators to account without undermining it.

**Objectives**

- Explain the rationale for operational independence and the risks of political interference
- Design governance, appointment, funding and legal protection arrangements
- Apply accountability tools such as reporting, performance review and parliamentary scrutiny

**Syllabus**

- Independence in international standards: what is expected
- Board composition, appointments, dismissal and funding models
- Annual reports, remit letters, performance frameworks and audit
- Questions government should ask its regulators

### Module 5: International Standards, Standard-Setters and Peer Reviews

The international bodies that set financial standards, how those standards bind or influence national policy, and how assessments and peer reviews judge a country's framework.

**Objectives**

- Describe the roles of the G20, FSB, BCBS, IOSCO, IAIS, CPMI and FATF
- Explain how standards are developed and how government can influence them
- Prepare for IMF and World Bank assessments, FSB peer reviews and FATF evaluations

**Syllabus**

- The international financial architecture and its standard-setters
- Core principles for banking, insurance, securities and market infrastructure
- Financial sector assessments, peer reviews and mutual evaluations
- Implementing recommendations and managing reputational consequences

### Module 6: Banking, Insurance and Prudential Regulation

How banks and insurers are regulated for safety and soundness, what capital and liquidity rules aim to achieve, and the policy choices government faces in implementing them.

**Objectives**

- Explain the purpose of capital, liquidity and solvency requirements
- Assess proportionality choices for smaller and domestic institutions
- Identify the fiscal and growth implications of prudential policy

**Syllabus**

- Capital, leverage and liquidity standards for banks
- Solvency and policyholder protection for insurers
- Proportionality, local adaptation and timing of implementation
- Credit supply, lending to the economy and the cost of prudential rules

### Module 7: Capital Markets, Conduct and Consumer Protection

How securities markets, investment services and retail financial products are regulated, and the policy choices in balancing investor protection, market access and innovation.

**Objectives**

- Explain the regulation of issuers, intermediaries, funds and trading venues
- Describe conduct and consumer protection frameworks and redress mechanisms
- Assess where conduct failures create political and fiscal pressure

**Syllabus**

- Securities regulation: disclosure, market abuse and intermediaries
- Retail investor protection, suitability and product governance
- Complaints, ombudsman schemes and compensation arrangements
- Case studies of mis-selling scandals and the policy response

### Module 8: Financial Stability, Crisis Management and Financial Crime

The macroprudential framework, how failing institutions are resolved, and the national arrangements for combating money laundering, terrorist financing and sanctions evasion.

**Objectives**

- Explain systemic risk and the macroprudential tools available to authorities
- Describe resolution regimes, deposit insurance and the role of the finance ministry in a crisis
- Identify government's responsibilities for AML/CFT and sanctions

**Syllabus**

- Systemic risk, macroprudential tools and financial stability committees
- Resolution, bail-in, deposit insurance and the limits of public support
- National AML/CFT strategy, FATF standards and the national FIU
- Financial sanctions: implementation and enforcement

### Module 9: Policy Levers and Policy Design

The instruments government holds over the financial sector, from legislation and regulatory remits to tax, public ownership and market development, and how to design and evaluate policy using them.

**Objectives**

- Identify the legislative, fiscal, institutional and convening levers available
- Apply cost-benefit analysis and consultation to financial services policy
- Evaluate industry proposals and lobbying with appropriate scepticism

**Syllabus**

- Legislation, secondary rules and remit letters
- Tax policy, public guarantees, state-owned institutions and development banks
- Impact assessment, consultation and post-implementation review
- Managing industry engagement and avoiding regulatory capture

### Module 10: Designing a National Financial Services Policy

Brings the programme together by examining how a national financial services strategy is developed, how emerging issues are anticipated and how policy is translated into action.

**Objectives**

- Assess the national financial sector's strengths, vulnerabilities and priorities
- Anticipate emerging issues including digital assets, AI, climate and cyber risk
- Produce a policy action plan with clear roles for government and regulators

**Syllabus**

- Diagnosing the financial sector: evidence, data and assessments
- Emerging issues: digital assets, AI, sustainable finance and operational resilience
- Sequencing reforms and building institutional capacity
- Workshop: a national financial services policy action plan

## Structure and Format

Ten modules of about three hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as an intensive programme over three to four days or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a plain-language briefing with case studies and policy discussion, and modules can be selected or tailored to the ministry's priorities.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some experience of public policy or economic affairs is helpful. No technical background in finance or regulation is required.

## Core Reading

- BCBS (2024), Core Principles for Effective Banking Supervision
- IOSCO (2017), Objectives and Principles of Securities Regulation
- IAIS (2019), Insurance Core Principles and Common Framework for the Supervision of Internationally Active Insurance Groups
- IMF (2021), Financial Sector Assessment Program Review: Towards a More Stable and Sustainable Financial System
