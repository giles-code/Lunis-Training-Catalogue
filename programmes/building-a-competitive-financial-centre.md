## Aim and Purpose

To give officials responsible for financial centre development a practical framework for positioning a jurisdiction, attracting financial business and building the legal, tax, talent and regulatory foundations that sustain it. Participants leave able to design a credible strategy, understand how international firms choose where to locate, avoid the reputational traps of competing on lax standards, and measure the results.

**Coverage:** What makes a financial centre competitive, strategic positioning and niche selection, regulatory reputation and the role of the regulator, licensing speed versus standards, legal, tax and market infrastructure, talent and immigration, attracting fund managers, fintechs and digital asset firms, international standards and blacklists, and measuring success.

## Intended Audience

- Staff of investment promotion agencies, financial centre development bodies and economic development boards
- Officials in finance and economy ministries responsible for financial sector growth strategy
- Senior officials and advisers who oversee financial centre strategy and its relationship with the regulator

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain the factors that determine where financial firms choose to locate
- Assess a jurisdiction's competitive strengths, weaknesses and credible niches
- Evaluate the trade-offs between licensing speed, regulatory standards and reputation
- Identify the legal, tax, talent and infrastructure foundations a financial centre needs
- Design an investment promotion approach for fund managers, fintechs and other target sectors
- Define success measures and an action plan for financial centre strategy

## Syllabus

### Module 1: What Makes a Financial Centre Competitive

The economics of financial centres, how international firms take location decisions, and the factors that distinguish global, regional and specialist centres.

**Objectives**

- Explain the agglomeration economics behind financial centres
- Describe how firms decide where to locate people, entities and activities
- Distinguish global, regional, offshore and niche financial centres

**Syllabus**

- Agglomeration, clusters and network effects
- Location decisions: market access, talent, cost, regulation and tax
- Financial centre indices: what they measure and what they miss
- Case studies of centres that grew, specialised or declined

### Module 2: Strategy and Positioning

How to develop a financial centre strategy grounded in evidence, choose credible niches and align ministries, agencies, the regulator and industry behind it.

**Objectives**

- Diagnose the jurisdiction's current position using data and stakeholder evidence
- Select target sectors and niches that build on genuine strengths
- Design governance for the strategy across government, regulator and industry

**Syllabus**

- Diagnostic tools: sector mapping, benchmarking and competitor analysis
- Niche selection: funds, insurance, fintech, digital assets and sustainable finance
- Public-private partnership models and industry bodies
- Avoiding over-reliance on a single sector or source of business

### Module 3: Regulatory Reputation and the Role of the Regulator

Why regulatory quality and independence are a competitive asset, how reputation is built and lost, and how government should frame the regulator's role in growth without compromising its independence.

**Objectives**

- Explain why international firms and investors value a credible regulator
- Assess the risks of competitiveness or growth objectives for regulators
- Define a constructive relationship between promotion bodies and the regulator

**Syllabus**

- Regulatory reputation as the foundation of market access
- Growth and competitiveness objectives: design and safeguards
- Separation of promotion and supervision functions
- Case studies of reputational damage from regulatory failure

### Module 4: Licensing Speed, Standards and the Race to the Bottom

How to make authorisation efficient and predictable without lowering standards, and why competing on light-touch licensing attracts the wrong firms and invites international scrutiny.

**Objectives**

- Identify what makes authorisation slow and what genuinely speeds it up
- Evaluate service standards, pre-application engagement and fast-track routes
- Assess the risks of attracting firms that seek weak oversight

**Syllabus**

- Authorisation service standards and transparency
- Pre-application support, innovation hubs and sandboxes
- Substance requirements and avoiding letterbox entities
- Signals of a race to the bottom and how standard-setters respond

### Module 5: Legal, Tax and Market Infrastructure

The legal, judicial, tax and market infrastructure that international firms expect, from flexible corporate and fund vehicles to treaty networks, courts and trading and settlement systems.

**Objectives**

- Identify the legal vehicles and judicial arrangements firms look for
- Explain tax neutrality, treaty networks and international tax standards
- Assess the market infrastructure and connectivity a centre requires

**Syllabus**

- Company, partnership, trust and fund vehicle legislation
- Commercial courts, arbitration and insolvency regimes
- Tax neutrality, treaties, global minimum tax and transparency standards
- Exchanges, settlement, custody and digital infrastructure

### Module 6: Talent, Skills and Quality of Life

How access to skilled people determines a centre's growth, and the immigration, education, professional development and quality-of-life policies that attract and retain talent.

**Objectives**

- Assess the talent needs of target sectors and current gaps
- Evaluate immigration, visa and professional recognition policies
- Design skills pipelines with universities, industry and professional bodies

**Syllabus**

- Talent mapping for funds, fintech, compliance and technology roles
- Work permits, fast-track visas and recognition of qualifications
- Education, apprenticeships and professional certification
- Cost of living, connectivity and quality of life as location factors

### Module 7: Attracting Fund Managers, Fintechs and Digital Asset Firms

Practical investment promotion for priority sectors: understanding each sector's decision-making, tailoring the proposition, and conducting due diligence on the firms being attracted.

**Objectives**

- Explain what fund managers, fintechs and digital asset firms look for
- Design a targeted value proposition and promotion campaign
- Apply integrity and reputational due diligence to prospective firms

**Syllabus**

- Fund domiciliation, management and servicing decisions
- Fintech and digital asset firm location drivers
- Promotion tools: missions, events, landing support and incentives
- Integrity checks on promoters, beneficial owners and business models

### Module 8: International Standards, Reputation and Measuring Success

Brings the programme together by examining how international lists, assessments and peer reviews affect a centre's reputation, how success should be measured, and what a credible action plan contains.

**Objectives**

- Assess the impact of FATF, tax transparency and financial sector assessments on reputation
- Define meaningful success measures beyond licence numbers
- Produce a financial centre strategy action plan

**Syllabus**

- FATF lists, tax non-cooperative lists and their commercial consequences
- Indicators: jobs, tax, exports, substance and assets under management
- Monitoring, evaluation and reporting to ministers
- Workshop: a financial centre strategy and action plan

## Structure and Format

Eight modules of about three hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as an intensive programme over two to three days or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a briefing with comparative case studies and practical exercises, ending in a strategy workshop, and modules can be selected or tailored to the jurisdiction's target sectors.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some experience of economic development, investment promotion or financial services policy is helpful. No technical background in regulation is required.

## Core Reading

- IOSCO (2017), Objectives and Principles of Securities Regulation

- OECD (2021), Statement on a Two-Pillar Solution to Address the Tax Challenges Arising from the Digitalisation of the Economy
- OECD (2023), OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises on Responsible Business Conduct
