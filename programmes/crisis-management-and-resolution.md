## Aim and Purpose

To build the capability authorities need for the moments that matter most but occur least, from early intervention through recovery and resolution to system-wide stress. Participants leave able to recognise distress early, escalate with confidence, judge the credibility of recovery and resolution plans, and make and communicate decisions under time pressure.

**Coverage:** The authority's role in a crisis, early warning indicators, the intervention ladder, recovery planning, resolution planning and resolvability, resolution tools and orderly failure, systemic risk and contagion, macroprudential tools, crisis communication and coordination, and a firm-distress simulation.

## Intended Audience

- Supervisors and risk specialists responsible for firms that may come under stress
- Resolution, financial stability and macroprudential staff
- Senior managers and executives who would lead or take decisions in the authority's crisis response

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain the roles of supervisors, resolution authorities and other bodies in a financial crisis
- Identify early warning signs and apply a structured intervention ladder
- Assess the credibility of recovery plans and the resolvability of firms
- Evaluate systemic risk, contagion channels and the macroprudential tools to address them
- Communicate and coordinate effectively with the public, industry, government and other authorities
- Make and evaluate supervisory decisions under time pressure in a simulated crisis

## Syllabus

### Module 1: Financial Crises and the Authority's Role

How firms and markets fail, the roles of the supervisor, resolution authority, central bank and protection schemes, and how an authority organises itself to take crisis decisions.

**Objectives**

- Explain the common paths from stress to failure
- Describe the roles of the authorities involved in a crisis
- Assess the authority's own crisis governance and readiness

**Syllabus**

- Anatomy of firm failure: solvency, liquidity, conduct and operational triggers
- Supervisors, resolution authorities, central banks and protection schemes
- FSB Key Attributes and IADI principles
- Crisis governance: decision rights, playbooks and escalation within the authority

### Module 2: Early Warning Indicators and Signs of Distress

The quantitative and behavioural signals that typically precede firm distress, and how supervisors build them into monitoring so problems are spotted while there is still time to act.

**Objectives**

- Identify common early warning indicators across risk types
- Distinguish behavioural from quantitative warning signs
- Assess whether monitoring arrangements would detect distress in time

**Syllabus**

- Capital, liquidity, earnings and funding indicators
- Behavioural signals: management turnover, evasiveness and delayed reporting
- Market signals, complaints and whistleblowing
- Early warning dashboards and their limits

### Module 3: The Intervention Ladder: Escalation and Early Action

How a structured intervention ladder makes escalation consistent and defensible, how to set decision thresholds, and the options available at each rung before problems become crises.

**Objectives**

- Explain the intervention ladder from monitoring to formal action
- Apply decision thresholds while balancing false alarms against late action
- Formulate an early intervention response for a firm in difficulty

**Syllabus**

- Why early intervention is more effective and less costly
- Thresholds, triggers and documenting escalation decisions
- Options at each rung: enhanced monitoring to restrictions and removals
- Case study: a firm showing early signs of distress

### Module 4: Recovery Planning: Assessing Credibility

How to assess whether a firm's recovery plan would genuinely restore viability under severe stress, and the common weaknesses supervisors should challenge.

**Objectives**

- Explain the purpose and content of a recovery plan
- Assess recovery indicators, options and scenarios for credibility
- Challenge unrealistic assumptions and untested options

**Syllabus**

- Recovery plans: governance, indicators, options and communication
- Severe but plausible scenarios and the capacity of recovery options
- Common weaknesses: optimistic timing, value and executability
- Exercise: assessing a sample recovery plan

### Module 5: Resolution Planning and Resolvability

The distinction between firm-led recovery and authority-led resolution, the supervisor's role in resolution planning, and how to identify and remove barriers to resolvability in advance.

**Objectives**

- Distinguish recovery planning from resolution planning
- Describe the supervisor's contribution to resolution planning
- Evaluate resolvability barriers in structure, data and continuity of services

**Syllabus**

- Preferred resolution strategies and the role of the resolution authority
- Barriers: legal structure, intra-group dependencies, data and systems
- Operational continuity and critical shared services
- Case study: a firm with significant resolvability barriers

### Module 6: Resolution Tools and Orderly Failure

The tools available to resolve or wind down a failing firm, how they apply across banks, insurers, investment firms and other sectors, and how customers and critical functions are protected.

**Objectives**

- Explain the main resolution and wind-down tools and when each is used
- Assess how customer assets, deposits and policyholders are protected
- Evaluate the practical steps in executing a resolution or orderly wind-down

**Syllabus**

- Transfer, bridge institution, bail-in and run-off
- Insolvency, special administration and solvent wind-down
- Deposit guarantee, investor and policyholder protection and client assets
- Resolution weekend: valuation, sequencing and decision-making

### Module 7: Systemic Risk and Contagion

What distinguishes systemic from firm-specific risk, how stress spreads between firms and markets, and how authorities identify interconnectedness before it crystallises.

**Objectives**

- Explain the drivers of systemic risk, including beyond the banking sector
- Describe contagion channels between firms and markets
- Identify systemic risk indicators and the data needed to monitor them

**Syllabus**

- Interconnectedness, size and substitutability
- Contagion: direct exposures, fire sales and confidence effects
- Non-bank financial intermediation and cross-border spillovers
- Exercise: mapping contagion channels in an interconnected market

### Module 8: Macroprudential Tools and System-Wide Monitoring

How authorities monitor the build-up of system-wide vulnerabilities and choose among macroprudential tools, matching each tool to the risk it is designed to address.

**Objectives**

- Design a systemic risk dashboard drawing on firm and market data
- Assess the main macroprudential tools and their trade-offs
- Match tools to specific systemic risk drivers and decide when to deploy them

**Syllabus**

- Systemic risk dashboards and system-wide stress testing
- Countercyclical and systemic capital buffers
- Sectoral capital requirements and borrower-based measures
- Case study: an emerging systemic risk and the policy response

### Module 9: Crisis Communication and Coordination

How to communicate clearly and quickly with the public, industry and government during a crisis, balance transparency against stability, and coordinate messages and actions across authorities.

**Objectives**

- Explain how crisis communication differs from routine communication
- Draft clear public statements under time pressure
- Coordinate messages and actions across domestic and cross-border authorities

**Syllabus**

- Transparency versus financial stability; risks of premature disclosure
- Statements, spokespeople and differentiated stakeholder messaging
- Crisis management groups and cross-border cooperation agreements
- Exercise: drafting a statement against the clock

### Module 10: Crisis Simulation: Firm-Specific Distress

A live simulation in which teams respond to an unfolding firm-distress scenario, making supervisory decisions, escalating and coordinating across functions under time pressure, followed by a structured debrief.

**Objectives**

- Apply decision-making frameworks under time pressure and incomplete information
- Coordinate a response across supervision, legal, resolution and communications
- Evaluate decisions made and identify lessons for crisis preparedness

**Syllabus**

- Scenario briefing, roles and initial rapid assessment
- Escalation, coordination and stakeholder management as the scenario evolves
- Facilitator-led debrief comparing team decisions
- Workshop: the authority's crisis preparedness action plan

## Structure and Format

Ten modules of about three hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a multi-day programme or as a series of half-day sessions. Modules combine short input with case studies and exercises, ending in a live crisis simulation and debrief, and can be selected or tailored to the authority's sectors and resolution framework.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some experience of supervision, financial stability or risk work is helpful. No prior crisis or resolution experience is required.

## Core Reading

- FSB, Key Attributes of Effective Resolution Regimes for Financial Institutions (2014)
- Basel Committee, Guidelines for Identifying and Dealing with Weak Banks (2015)
- IADI, Core Principles for Effective Deposit Insurance Systems
