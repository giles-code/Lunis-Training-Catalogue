## Aim and Purpose

To give technology, security, risk and business leaders a practical, non-mathematical grounding in the quantum threat and what it means for their firm's systems, data and third parties. Participants leave able to scope a cryptographic inventory, prioritise exposure, plan a credible multi-year migration and report quantum readiness to the board and the authority.

**Coverage:** Quantum computing and the cryptographic threat, harvest now decrypt later, where cryptography sits in the firm, building a cryptographic inventory, risk assessment and prioritisation, NIST post-quantum standards and crypto-agility, migration planning and delivery, vendors, payment and market infrastructures and digital assets, and governance, board reporting and supervisory engagement.

## Intended Audience

- Chief information security officers, chief technology officers, architects and security engineering leads
- Operational resilience, technology risk, third-party risk and internal audit staff
- Senior managers and programme sponsors accountable for cyber, data protection and change

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain in plain language why quantum computing threatens public-key cryptography and why the firm should act now
- Identify where cryptography sits across the firm's systems, data, products and third parties
- Build a cryptographic inventory and prioritise systems by exposure, data shelf life and criticality
- Plan a credible, crypto-agile migration to the NIST post-quantum standards
- Manage dependencies on vendors, payment schemes, market infrastructures and digital asset networks
- Govern the programme and report quantum readiness to the board and supervisors

## Syllabus

### Module 1: Quantum Computing and the Cryptographic Threat

A plain-language introduction to quantum computing, why it breaks today's public-key cryptography, the uncertainty around timelines and why data harvested now may be decrypted later.

**Objectives**

- Explain how quantum computers differ from classical computers without the mathematics
- Distinguish the impact of quantum attacks on public-key and symmetric cryptography
- Assess the firm's harvest now, decrypt later exposure from long-lived data

**Syllabus**

- Qubits, error correction and cryptographically relevant machines
- Why public-key encryption and digital signatures are most at risk
- Timeline estimates and why uncertainty is not a reason to wait
- Harvest now, decrypt later and the shelf life of client data

### Module 2: Where Cryptography Sits in Your Firm

A map of where cryptography protects a financial services firm, from client channels and trading links to payment messaging, hardware security modules, certificates and signatures on ledgers.

**Objectives**

- Identify the main uses of cryptography in the firm's business services
- Recognise cryptography embedded in legacy, cloud and vendor systems
- Link cryptographic dependencies to important business services and client data

**Syllabus**

- Secure communications, certificates and public key infrastructure
- Client portals, trading connectivity and payment messaging
- Hardware security modules, key management and digital signatures
- Cryptography hidden in vendor products, libraries and cloud services

### Module 3: Building the Cryptographic Inventory

How to discover and record where and how cryptography is used across the firm and its supply chain, who owns the inventory and how to keep it complete and decision-useful.

**Objectives**

- Design the scope, data fields and ownership of a cryptographic inventory
- Combine discovery tools, architecture review and vendor information
- Test the inventory for completeness, including third-party and embedded uses

**Syllabus**

- Discovery tools, manual review and the cryptographic bill of materials
- Data fields: algorithm, key length, location, owner and dependency
- Gathering information from vendors and outsourced providers
- Common gaps: partial scope, no named owner and stale records

### Module 4: Risk Assessment and Prioritisation

How to turn the inventory into a risk-based view of quantum exposure, ranking systems by data sensitivity and shelf life, business criticality and difficulty of change, within the firm's risk appetite.

**Objectives**

- Assess quantum exposure using data shelf life, sensitivity and system criticality
- Prioritise remediation by risk and by time and effort to migrate
- Integrate quantum risk into the firm's cyber, technology and operational risk frameworks

**Syllabus**

- Exposure scoring: data shelf life, migration time and threat timeline
- Linking to important business services and impact tolerances
- Risk appetite, risk register entries and key risk indicators
- Case study: prioritising a firm's systems for migration

### Module 5: Post-Quantum Standards and Crypto-Agility

The NIST post-quantum standards, how hybrid approaches work during transition, and why designing for crypto-agility matters as much as choosing the right algorithm.

**Objectives**

- Explain the NIST post-quantum standards and their intended uses
- Evaluate hybrid deployment, performance impacts and interoperability
- Apply crypto-agility as a design principle in architecture and procurement

**Syllabus**

- NIST post-quantum standards: ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA
- Hybrid schemes during transition and their trade-offs
- Performance, key sizes and interoperability testing
- Crypto-agility in architecture, coding standards and procurement

### Module 6: Planning and Running the Migration Programme

What a realistic multi-year migration programme involves, from roadmap and resourcing to testing, sequencing and integration with the firm's wider technology change and refresh cycles.

**Objectives**

- Design a migration roadmap with milestones, dependencies and resourcing
- Sequence migration to protect high-value data and critical services first
- Manage testing, change control and rollback for cryptographic change

**Syllabus**

- Roadmaps, milestones and alignment with technology refresh cycles
- Business case, budget and skills for a multi-year programme
- Testing, change management and rollback
- Measuring progress: coverage, exceptions and residual risk

### Module 7: Vendors, Infrastructures and Digital Assets

Why most firms depend on vendors, cloud providers, payment schemes and market infrastructures to migrate, and the particular quantum challenges for blockchains, wallets and digital asset custody.

**Objectives**

- Assess vendor and cloud provider post-quantum roadmaps and contractual commitments
- Coordinate migration with payment schemes, market infrastructures and counterparties
- Evaluate quantum risks to blockchain signatures, wallets and custody of digital assets

**Syllabus**

- Vendor due diligence, contract clauses and supply chain assurance
- Coordinated migration across payment and settlement systems
- Exposed public keys, wallet design and blockchain upgrade risk
- Implications for crypto-asset custody and tokenised assets

### Module 8: Governance, Board Reporting and Supervisory Engagement

Brings the programme together into a governance and reporting model for quantum readiness: accountability, board information, assurance and the evidence the firm should be able to show the authority.

**Objectives**

- Allocate accountability for quantum readiness across the three lines of defence
- Specify the management information and assurance the board needs
- Prepare the evidence of quantum readiness that supervisors will expect

**Syllabus**

- Ownership, steering groups and the role of internal audit
- Board reporting: inventory coverage, milestones, exceptions and dependencies
- International work: BIS, G7 Cyber Expert Group and national cyber agencies
- Workshop: the firm's quantum readiness plan and board paper

## Structure and Format

Eight modules of about three hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a four-day programme or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a briefing with case studies and practical exercises using inventory and roadmap templates, and modules can be selected or tailored to the firm's technology estate.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some experience of information security, technology risk or operational resilience is helpful. No knowledge of quantum physics or cryptographic mathematics is required.

## Core Reading

- NIST, FIPS 203, 204 and 205: Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards (2024)
- G7 Cyber Expert Group, Public Statement on Planning for the Opportunities and Risks of Quantum Computing (2024)
- NIST, Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024)
- FSB, Enhancing Third-Party Risk Management and Oversight: A Toolkit for Financial Institutions and Financial Authorities (2023)
