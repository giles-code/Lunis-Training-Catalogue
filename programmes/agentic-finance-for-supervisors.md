## Aim and Purpose

To give supervisors a clear understanding of how AI agents are beginning to act for firms and consumers, and how existing supervisory frameworks apply when software, not people, initiates transactions and decisions. Participants leave able to challenge firms on agent governance, identity, authority and controls, identify red flags and contribute to the authority's policy response.

**Coverage:** AI agents and levels of autonomy, the four-layer architecture of agentic finance, use cases and market structure, know your agent and trust frameworks, governance of autonomous decisions, agent-initiated payments and settlement, agents in markets, resilience and security, financial crime, conduct and liability, and supervisory strategy and policy.

## Intended Audience

- Supervisors of banks, payment and e-money firms, investment firms and crypto-asset service providers adopting AI agents
- Policy, innovation, payments and financial crime specialists assessing agent-driven business models
- Senior managers shaping the authority's response to AI and autonomous finance

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain how AI agents work, what makes them different from earlier automation and where they are entering finance
- Analyse agentic finance through its governance, decision, asset and settlement layers
- Assess how firms establish agent identity, delegated authority and trust
- Evaluate governance, resilience and market integrity risks where agents act with limited human intervention
- Identify financial crime, consumer protection and liability issues in agent-initiated transactions
- Design a supervisory and policy response to agentic finance, drawing on FSB and other international work

## Syllabus

### Module 1: From Automation to Agency: AI Agents in Finance

What AI agents are, how they plan, use tools and act on goals, and why delegating decisions and transactions to software changes the supervisory picture.

**Objectives**

- Explain how AI agents combine models, tools, memory and goals
- Distinguish agents from rules-based automation, algorithms and chatbots
- Identify the levels of autonomy at which agents operate

**Syllabus**

- Large language models, tool use and autonomous task execution
- Levels of autonomy: assistive, supervised and fully delegated agents
- Single-agent and multi-agent systems and agent-to-agent interaction
- Capabilities, limits and failure modes: hallucination, goal drift and overreach

### Module 2: The Architecture of the Agentic Era

A four-layer model of agentic finance, covering governance, decision, asset and settlement layers, and how it helps supervisors locate risk, control and accountability.

**Objectives**

- Explain the governance, decision, asset and settlement layers of agentic finance
- Analyse how risk and control points shift between the layers
- Map existing regulatory requirements onto each layer

**Syllabus**

- Governance layer: mandates, permissions, policies and oversight
- Decision layer: models, reasoning and recommendations
- Asset layer: accounts, wallets, tokenised assets and custody
- Settlement layer: payment rails, stablecoins and programmable money

### Module 3: Use Cases and Changing Market Structure

Where agents are already appearing in financial services, from agent-led shopping and treasury to trading and personal finance, and the new intermediaries and dependencies this creates.

**Objectives**

- Identify current and emerging agentic use cases across sectors
- Analyse the business models of agent providers, platforms and incumbent firms
- Evaluate how agents could change competition, distribution and concentration

**Syllabus**

- Agentic commerce and consumer-facing financial agents
- Corporate treasury, operations and back-office agents
- Agents in trading, investment and personal financial management
- New intermediaries: agent platforms, tool providers and agent marketplaces

### Module 4: Know Your Agent: Identity, Authority and Trust

How firms verify which agent they are dealing with, whom it acts for and what it is allowed to do, and the trust frameworks emerging to make agent identity and delegated authority verifiable.

**Objectives**

- Explain the know your agent concept and how it extends customer due diligence
- Assess how firms verify agent identity, principal and scope of authority
- Evaluate emerging trust frameworks, credentials and registries for agents

**Syllabus**

- Binding agents to principals: verifiable credentials and digital identity
- Delegated authority: mandates, spending limits, scopes and revocation
- Trust frameworks, agent registries and interoperability standards
- Red flags: anonymous agents, unbounded mandates and impersonation

### Module 5: Governing Autonomous Decision-Making in Firms

What sound governance looks like when agents make or execute decisions for a firm or its customers, and how supervisors test accountability, human oversight and control in practice.

**Objectives**

- Assess accountability for agent decisions at board and senior management level
- Evaluate guardrails, human oversight and escalation arrangements
- Apply model risk and testing expectations to agentic systems

**Syllabus**

- Senior manager accountability and agent inventories
- Human-in-the-loop, human-on-the-loop and meaningful oversight
- Guardrails, permissions, audit trails and kill-switches
- Pre-deployment testing, red-teaming and ongoing monitoring

### Module 6: Payments and Settlement by Agents

How agents initiate and settle payments across card, account-to-account, tokenised and stablecoin rails, and what this means for authentication, consent, fraud and liability allocation.

**Objectives**

- Explain how agent-initiated payments work across traditional and tokenised rails
- Assess consent, authentication and authorisation where no human acts at the point of payment
- Evaluate how liability for unauthorised or erroneous agent payments is allocated

**Syllabus**

- Agent-initiated payments: tokenised credentials, mandates and payment APIs
- Strong authentication and consent in delegated transactions
- Stablecoins, tokenised deposits and programmable settlement for agents
- Disputes, refunds and liability between consumer, agent provider and firm

### Module 7: Agents in Markets: Trading, Advice and Market Integrity

The market integrity and conduct risks when agents trade, allocate portfolios and advise or act for clients, including herding, collusion, manipulation and suitability.

**Objectives**

- Analyse how agents could amplify herding, volatility and correlated behaviour
- Identify risks of emergent collusion and manipulation between agents
- Assess suitability and best interest obligations where agents act for clients

**Syllabus**

- Agent-driven trading, execution and portfolio management
- Herding, flash events and procyclicality from similar models
- Algorithmic collusion and agent-driven manipulation
- Suitability, best execution and conflicts in agent-mediated advice

### Module 8: Resilience, Security and Third-Party Risk in Agentic Systems

How agentic systems can fail or be attacked, how failures cascade across connected agents, and how dependence on a few model and platform providers concentrates risk.

**Objectives**

- Identify attack vectors specific to agents, including prompt injection and tool misuse
- Assess cascading failure and contagion risk in multi-agent systems
- Evaluate concentration, outsourcing and exit risk on model and agent providers

**Syllabus**

- Prompt injection, credential theft and compromised tools
- Cascading failures and runaway agents: containment and recovery
- Third-party and foundation model concentration risk
- Incident reporting, resilience testing and the supervisory evidence to request

### Module 9: Financial Crime, Conduct and Liability

How agents can be exploited for fraud, money laundering and sanctions evasion, how consumer protection applies when agents act for customers, and who is accountable when things go wrong.

**Objectives**

- Identify financial crime typologies that exploit autonomous agents
- Assess customer due diligence, monitoring and sanctions controls for agent activity
- Evaluate consumer protection and liability where agents act for customers

**Syllabus**

- Agent-enabled fraud, synthetic identities and scams at scale
- Money laundering and sanctions evasion through agent networks and crypto rails
- Fair treatment, vulnerable customers and manipulation of consumer agents
- Liability chains: principal, agent provider, platform and regulated firm

### Module 10: Supervising the Agentic Era: Strategy and Policy

Brings the programme together into the authority's strategic response, covering international work on AI, perimeter and liability policy, supervisory tools and the authority's own use of agents.

**Objectives**

- Compare international approaches to AI and agentic finance, including FSB work
- Evaluate perimeter, liability and trust framework policy options
- Design a supervisory roadmap for agentic finance

**Syllabus**

- International work: FSB, IOSCO, BIS, CPMI, FATF and OECD
- Perimeter questions: when an agent provider becomes a regulated activity
- Sandboxes, industry engagement and supervisory use of agents
- Workshop: the authority's agentic finance roadmap

## Structure and Format

Ten modules of about three hours each, grouped into four pathways and delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a multi-day programme or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a briefing with scenario exercises, red flags and supervisory questions, and pathways or individual modules can be selected or tailored to the authority's needs.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some understanding of AI in financial services, for example from AI Governance for Supervisors, is helpful. No technical background is required.

## Core Reading

- FSB, The Financial Stability Implications of Artificial Intelligence (2024)
- OECD, Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence (2019, revised 2024)
- NIST, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) (2023)
- FATF, Guidance on Digital Identity (2020)
