## Aim and Purpose

To give supervisors a practical understanding of how AI and machine learning are built and used in financial services, and the analytical tools to assess whether firms govern them well. Participants leave able to probe firms' AI governance, model risk and customer outcomes, identify red flags and contribute to the authority's own approach to AI supervision.

**Coverage:** AI and machine learning foundations, governance and ethics, explainability and model risk, bias and fairness, AI in trading and operations, AI in advice and consumer services, AI vulnerabilities and failure modes, AI regulation and the financial perimeter, SupTech, and building the authority's approach to AI risk.

## Intended Audience

- Supervisors and inspectors responsible for overseeing AI use by regulated firms across all sectors
- Risk, policy and technology specialists working on digital finance, model risk or innovation
- Senior managers and heads of department shaping the authority's approach to AI supervision

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain how AI and machine learning systems are developed, deployed and fail in financial services
- Assess the adequacy of a firm's AI governance, accountability and oversight arrangements
- Evaluate model risk, explainability, bias and fairness in AI-driven decisions
- Challenge firms on AI risks in trading, operations, advice and consumer-facing services
- Apply AI regulation and international standards to the financial regulatory perimeter
- Design an institution-wide approach to AI supervision, including the authority's own use of SupTech

## Syllabus

### Module 1: Foundations of AI and Machine Learning

A grounding in how AI and machine learning systems are built, trained and deployed in financial services, how they differ from rules-based and statistical models, and why firms are adopting them.

**Objectives**

- Explain how machine learning models are developed, trained and deployed
- Distinguish traditional statistical models, machine learning and generative AI
- Identify the drivers and patterns of AI adoption across financial sectors

**Syllabus**

- Supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning in financial applications
- The AI lifecycle: data, training, validation, deployment and monitoring
- Generative AI and large language models: capabilities and limits
- In-house build versus vendor AI: supervisory implications

### Module 2: AI Governance Frameworks and Ethics

How firms should govern the design, deployment and use of AI, the ethical principles governance must address, and how supervisors assess a firm's governance maturity.

**Objectives**

- Explain the core components of an effective AI governance framework
- Assess how accountability for AI-driven decisions is allocated within a firm
- Evaluate a firm's AI governance against supervisory expectations

**Syllabus**

- Board and senior management accountability for AI systems
- AI policies, risk appetite statements and model inventories
- Ethical principles in practice: fairness, transparency and human oversight
- The three lines of defence and model validation applied to AI

### Module 3: Explainability, Transparency and Model Risk

Why complex AI models are hard to interpret, what explainability techniques can realistically deliver, and how model risk management should extend to machine learning and generative AI.

**Objectives**

- Explain the trade-off between model complexity and explainability
- Apply model risk management principles to AI and machine learning
- Evaluate a firm's model documentation, validation and monitoring

**Syllabus**

- Black-box models and interpretation techniques, from feature importance to counterfactuals
- Explainability expectations by use case, from credit decisions to trading
- Extending model risk frameworks to machine learning and generative AI
- Supervisory questions on drift, retraining and independent validation

### Module 4: Bias, Fairness and Discrimination

How bias enters AI systems and produces unfair customer outcomes, how fairness is measured and tested, and the supervisory tools for assessing fairness in AI-driven products and services.

**Objectives**

- Explain the sources of bias in data, model design and deployment
- Analyse the fair-treatment and non-discrimination implications of AI decisions
- Evaluate a firm's bias testing and fairness monitoring

**Syllabus**

- Historical data bias, proxy discrimination and feedback loops
- Fairness metrics and their trade-offs in credit scoring and insurance pricing
- Bias testing methodologies and ongoing outcome monitoring
- Case studies: AI bias, supervisory findings and remediation

### Module 5: AI in Trading, Investment and Operations

The use of AI in trading, investment management and back-office processing, and the supervisory risks that arise when AI systems act on markets and infrastructure at speed and scale.

**Objectives**

- Explain how AI is used in trading, portfolio construction and operations
- Analyse the market integrity risks of AI-driven trading
- Evaluate controls over AI systems operating with limited human intervention

**Syllabus**

- AI in algorithmic and high-frequency trading strategies
- Portfolio construction, signal generation and AI-driven risk tools
- Correlated behaviour, flash events and unintended algorithmic collusion
- Kill-switches, human oversight and escalation for autonomous systems

### Module 6: AI in Advice and Consumer-Facing Services

The growing use of AI in robo-advice, chatbots, personalisation and pricing, and the suitability, disclosure and fair-treatment questions supervisors should put to firms.

**Objectives**

- Explain how AI is used in digital advice and customer engagement
- Assess the suitability and disclosure implications of AI-driven recommendations
- Evaluate consumer protection risks from personalisation and automated advice

**Syllabus**

- Robo-advice and automated portfolio recommendation engines
- Chatbots and virtual assistants: accuracy, complaints and vulnerable customers
- Personalisation and dynamic pricing: fair-treatment implications
- The guidance versus regulated advice boundary in AI-driven journeys

### Module 7: AI Vulnerabilities and Failure Modes

A practical framework for identifying how AI systems fail, are manipulated or behave unexpectedly, and the evidence supervisors should request to test a firm's resilience.

**Objectives**

- Identify common AI vulnerabilities and failure modes
- Analyse the operational, cyber and third-party risks specific to AI
- Apply a structured framework to probe firms on AI resilience

**Syllabus**

- Drift, data degradation and silent failure in production systems
- Adversarial attacks, data poisoning and prompt injection
- Vendor dependency and sector-wide concentration on AI providers
- Supervisory questions and evidence requests on AI resilience

### Module 8: AI Regulation and the Financial Regulatory Perimeter

How jurisdictions are regulating AI, how horizontal AI rules interact with existing financial services requirements, and what overlapping regimes mean for supervisory practice and coordination.

**Objectives**

- Explain the main models of AI regulation and the obligations they create
- Analyse how AI rules overlay conduct, prudential and consumer protection requirements
- Evaluate coordination and perimeter issues between financial supervisors and other authorities

**Syllabus**

- Approaches to AI regulation: risk-based laws, sectoral rules and supervisory guidance
- International standards and frameworks: OECD, NIST, ISO/IEC 42001, FSB, IOSCO and BIS
- High-risk financial use cases and existing sectoral obligations
- Coordination with data protection, competition and AI-specific authorities

### Module 9: SupTech: AI in Supervisory Practice

How supervisory authorities can use AI and data analytics in their own work, drawing on SupTech and RegTech case studies, and the governance and resourcing constraints they face.

**Objectives**

- Explain how RegTech and SupTech tools support compliance and supervision
- Analyse case studies of AI-enabled supervisory tools in practice
- Evaluate the opportunities and limits of AI adoption within the authority

**Syllabus**

- RegTech used by firms for compliance monitoring and reporting
- SupTech applications, including natural language processing of returns
- Anomaly detection, network analysis and risk dashboards
- Data quality, governance, skills and resourcing for supervisory AI

### Module 10: Building the Authority's Approach to AI Supervision

Brings the programme together into a strategic view of how the authority should supervise AI risk, balancing innovation against conduct, prudential and systemic risk, and building lasting capability.

**Objectives**

- Explain the strategic choices facing authorities as AI adoption accelerates
- Compare international approaches and coordination initiatives on AI in finance
- Design options for AI supervisory capability, policy and resourcing

**Syllabus**

- International coordination on AI: FSB, IOSCO and BIS workstreams
- Embedding AI oversight across existing supervisory functions
- Generative AI and autonomous agents: emerging supervisory questions
- Workshop: the authority's AI supervision roadmap

## Structure and Format

Ten modules of about three hours each, grouped into four pathways and delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a multi-day programme or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a technical briefing with case studies, red flags and supervisory question exercises, and pathways or individual modules can be selected or tailored to the authority's needs.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some experience of financial supervision or risk assessment is helpful. No technical background in AI or data science is required.

## Core Reading

- FSB, The Financial Stability Implications of Artificial Intelligence (2024)
- IOSCO, The Use of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning by Market Intermediaries and Asset Managers (2021)
- NIST, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) (2023)
- OECD, Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence (2019, revised 2024)
