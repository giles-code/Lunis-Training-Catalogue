## Aim and Purpose

To give supervisors and practitioners a practical command of the AI Act as it applies to banks, insurers, investment firms and other financial entities, and of how it fits alongside financial services rules and data protection law. Participants leave able to classify AI use cases, assess compliance programmes and anticipate how financial supervisors will act as market surveillance authorities.

**Coverage:** The AI Act's risk-based approach and scope, prohibited practices and transparency duties, high-risk systems in credit and life and health insurance, provider and deployer obligations, fundamental rights impact assessments, general-purpose AI models, governance through the AI Office and national authorities, the role of financial supervisors, interplay with financial services rules and the GDPR, and the phased timeline and building a compliance programme.

## Intended Audience

- Supervisors, policy and innovation staff at national competent authorities responsible for AI used by financial entities
- Compliance, risk, legal, data protection and model risk staff at banks, insurers, investment firms and payment firms
- Senior managers and directors accountable for the firm's use of AI and its AI Act compliance

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain the AI Act's risk-based approach, scope and key definitions as they apply to financial services
- Identify prohibited practices and classify financial use cases, including creditworthiness and insurance pricing
- Apply provider and deployer obligations, including risk management, data governance, human oversight and fundamental rights impact assessments
- Explain the obligations for general-purpose AI models and the governance role of the AI Office
- Assess how financial supervisors exercise market surveillance alongside financial services rules and data protection law
- Design a proportionate AI Act compliance programme integrated with the firm's existing governance

## Syllabus

### Module 1: The AI Act: Objectives, Scope and Risk-Based Approach

Why the EU legislated horizontally for AI, the definition of an AI system, material and territorial scope, the risk tiers, and how the Act borrows its architecture from EU product safety law.

**Objectives**

- Explain the AI Act's objectives and product-safety architecture
- Apply the definition of an AI system and the main scope exclusions
- Distinguish prohibited, high-risk, transparency-risk and minimal-risk systems

**Syllabus**

- Horizontal AI regulation, fundamental rights and the internal market
- The AI system definition, Commission guidelines and traditional statistical models
- Providers, deployers, importers, distributors and extraterritorial reach
- The risk tiers and the AI literacy obligation

### Module 2: Prohibited Practices and Transparency Obligations

The AI practices banned outright, those most relevant to financial services, and the transparency duties for chatbots, emotion recognition and AI-generated content used in customer journeys.

**Objectives**

- Identify prohibited practices and their relevance to financial services
- Apply transparency obligations to customer-facing AI and synthetic content
- Assess the controls that prevent prohibited uses within the firm

**Syllabus**

- Manipulative and exploitative techniques, social scoring and biometric categorisation
- Emotion recognition in the workplace and its limited exceptions
- Chatbot disclosure, deepfakes and labelling of AI-generated content
- Red flags in marketing, collections and customer engagement tools

### Module 3: High-Risk AI Systems in Financial Services

How AI systems are classified as high-risk, the financial use cases listed in Annex III, the filter for systems posing no significant risk, and how firms evidence and supervisors test classification decisions.

**Objectives**

- Apply the high-risk classification rules and the Annex III use cases
- Classify creditworthiness, credit scoring and life and health insurance pricing systems
- Document and challenge classification and derogation decisions

**Syllabus**

- Creditworthiness and credit scoring of individuals, and the fraud detection carve-out
- Risk assessment and pricing for life and health insurance
- Employment, biometric and other Annex III uses within financial firms
- Exercise: classifying a portfolio of financial AI use cases

### Module 4: Obligations of Providers and Deployers

The requirements for high-risk systems, who carries them, when a financial firm becomes a provider by building or substantially modifying a system, and deployer duties including human oversight and fundamental rights impact assessments.

**Objectives**

- Apply requirements on risk management, data governance, documentation, accuracy and robustness
- Determine when a firm is a provider, a deployer or both
- Conduct a fundamental rights impact assessment for credit and insurance uses

**Syllabus**

- Risk management system, data governance, technical documentation and logging
- Conformity assessment, registration and post-market monitoring
- Deployer duties: human oversight, input data, monitoring and informing individuals
- Fundamental rights impact assessments and the right to an explanation

### Module 5: General-Purpose AI Models and the Value Chain

The obligations on providers of general-purpose AI models, including models with systemic risk, the role of the code of practice, and what financial firms building on such models need from providers.

**Objectives**

- Explain general-purpose AI model obligations and the systemic-risk classification
- Assess the role of the code of practice and Commission guidelines
- Manage dependency on general-purpose models through procurement, contracts and testing

**Syllabus**

- Technical documentation, copyright policy and training content summaries
- Systemic-risk models: evaluation, serious incident reporting and cybersecurity
- The general-purpose AI code of practice and Commission guidelines
- Building on foundation models: value-chain information and DORA third-party risk

### Module 6: Governance, the AI Office and Financial Supervisors

The AI Act's governance structure, from the AI Office and AI Board to national market surveillance authorities, and how financial supervisors take on market surveillance for regulated financial entities.

**Objectives**

- Explain the roles of the AI Office, the AI Board, the scientific panel and national authorities
- Describe how financial supervisors act as market surveillance authorities for financial entities
- Apply the enforcement and penalty framework proportionately

**Syllabus**

- The AI Office, AI Board, advisory forum and scientific panel
- Financial supervisors as market surveillance authorities for regulated entities
- Cooperation with data protection authorities, the ESAs and prudential supervisors
- Investigations, corrective measures and administrative fines

### Module 7: Interplay with Financial Services Rules and Data Protection

How AI Act obligations integrate with internal governance, model risk, consumer credit, insurance distribution and DORA requirements, and with the GDPR's rules on automated decision-making and impact assessments.

**Objectives**

- Map AI Act obligations onto existing financial services governance requirements
- Apply the provisions that let financial firms meet obligations through internal governance rules
- Align AI Act compliance with the GDPR, including automated decisions and DPIAs

**Syllabus**

- Quality management and monitoring through internal governance arrangements
- Consumer credit, insurance distribution and conduct rules applied to AI
- GDPR: lawful basis, automated decision-making and DPIAs alongside FRIAs
- Model risk management, outsourcing and DORA as complementary frameworks

### Module 8: Implementation Timeline and Building a Compliance Programme

The phased application of the AI Act, the dependence on standards, guidance and possible simplification adjustments, and how to build or supervise a proportionate compliance programme, ending with a planning workshop.

**Objectives**

- Explain the phased application of the Act and the role of harmonised standards
- Design an AI inventory, classification process and compliance governance
- Prepare a roadmap for the firm's or authority's AI Act implementation

**Syllabus**

- Phased application: prohibitions, general-purpose models, high-risk and product-embedded systems
- Harmonised standards, guidance and proposed simplification adjustments
- AI inventory, ownership, policies, AI literacy and assurance
- Workshop: an AI Act implementation roadmap for the firm or authority

## Structure and Format

Eight modules of about three hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a multi-day programme or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a technical briefing with financial services case studies and classification exercises, and modules can be selected or tailored for supervisors or firms.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some familiarity with how AI or models are used in financial services is helpful. No technical or legal background is required.

## Core Reading

- Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act)
- Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on the protection of natural persons with regard to the processing of personal data and on the free movement of such data (General Data Protection Regulation)
- OECD, Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence (2019, revised 2024)
- FSB, The Financial Stability Implications of Artificial Intelligence (2024)
