## Aim and Purpose

To give supervisors a plain-language understanding of how quantum computing threatens the cryptography that secures payments, data and digital assets, and of what a credible migration to post-quantum cryptography involves. Participants leave able to assess firms' inventories, migration plans and third-party dependencies, identify red flags and contribute to the authority's quantum readiness strategy.

**Coverage:** Quantum computing and the cryptographic threat, harvest now decrypt later, where cryptography sits in financial services, cryptographic inventories and risk assessment, NIST post-quantum standards, migration planning and crypto-agility, third-party dependencies, impacts on payments and crypto-assets, and supervisory expectations.

## Intended Audience

- Supervisors and inspectors responsible for technology, cyber and operational resilience risk
- Payments, market infrastructure and digital asset specialists in supervisory and central bank functions
- Policy staff and senior managers shaping the authority's cyber and quantum readiness approach

## Learning Objectives

By the end of the programme, participants will be able to:

- Explain how quantum computing threatens public-key cryptography and why the risk is already present
- Identify where cryptography is embedded in financial services and which assets are most exposed
- Assess the completeness and quality of a firm's cryptographic inventory and risk assessment
- Evaluate post-quantum migration plans, crypto-agility and the use of NIST standards
- Analyse third-party, payments and crypto-asset dependencies that constrain migration
- Design proportionate supervisory expectations and a quantum readiness plan for the authority

## Syllabus

### Module 1: Quantum Computing and the Cryptographic Threat

A plain-language introduction to quantum computing, why it breaks today's public-key cryptography, the uncertainty around timelines and why data harvested now may be decrypted later.

**Objectives**

- Explain in plain language how quantum computers differ from classical computers
- Distinguish the impact of quantum attacks on public-key and symmetric cryptography
- Assess the harvest now, decrypt later risk and its link to data shelf life

**Syllabus**

- Qubits, error correction and the path to cryptographically relevant machines
- Why public-key encryption and digital signatures are most at risk
- Timeline estimates and why uncertainty is not a reason to wait
- Harvest now, decrypt later and long-lived confidential data

### Module 2: Where Cryptography Sits in Financial Services

A map of where cryptography protects the financial system, from customer channels and payment messaging to hardware security modules, certificates and digital signatures on contracts and ledgers.

**Objectives**

- Identify the main uses of cryptography across financial services
- Analyse which systems and data carry the highest quantum exposure
- Explain why cryptography is often invisible and poorly documented within firms

**Syllabus**

- Secure communications, certificates and public key infrastructure
- Payment cards, messaging networks and hardware security modules
- Digital signatures, identity and authentication
- Embedded cryptography in legacy and vendor systems

### Module 3: Cryptographic Inventories and Risk Assessment

How firms discover and record where and how cryptography is used, prioritise exposed systems and data, and how supervisors judge whether an inventory is complete and decision-useful.

**Objectives**

- Explain the purpose and contents of a cryptographic inventory
- Assess the completeness of a firm's inventory, including third-party and embedded uses
- Evaluate a firm's prioritisation of systems by exposure and data shelf life

**Syllabus**

- Discovery tools, manual review and the cryptographic bill of materials
- Prioritising by data sensitivity, shelf life and system criticality
- Governance: ownership, board reporting and risk appetite
- Red flags: partial inventories and no named owner

### Module 4: Post-Quantum Standards, Migration and Crypto-Agility

The NIST post-quantum standards, what a realistic multi-year migration programme involves, and why crypto-agility is as important as the choice of algorithm.

**Objectives**

- Explain the NIST post-quantum cryptography standards and their intended uses
- Assess the credibility, sequencing and resourcing of a firm's migration plan
- Evaluate a firm's approach to crypto-agility and hybrid deployment

**Syllabus**

- NIST post-quantum standards: ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA
- Migration roadmaps, milestones and testing
- Hybrid schemes, performance impacts and interoperability
- Crypto-agility as a design principle for future change

### Module 5: Third Parties, Payments and Crypto-Assets

Why most firms depend on vendors, payment schemes and market infrastructures to migrate, and the particular challenges quantum computing poses for blockchains, wallets and digital asset custody.

**Objectives**

- Assess firms' reliance on vendors and infrastructures for post-quantum migration
- Analyse coordination challenges in payment systems and market infrastructure
- Evaluate quantum risks to blockchain signatures, wallets and custody

**Syllabus**

- Vendor roadmaps, contractual commitments and supply chain assurance
- Coordinated migration across payment schemes and settlement systems
- Quantum exposure of blockchain signatures and exposed public keys
- Implications for crypto-asset custody and tokenised assets

### Module 6: Supervisory Expectations and Quantum Readiness

Brings the programme together into proportionate supervisory expectations for quantum readiness, the international work under way and an action plan for the authority's own systems.

**Objectives**

- Compare emerging supervisory and international approaches to quantum readiness
- Formulate proportionate supervisory expectations for different types of firm
- Design a quantum readiness plan for the authority and its regulated population

**Syllabus**

- International work: BIS, G7 Cyber Expert Group and national cyber agencies
- Embedding quantum risk in cyber and operational resilience supervision
- The authority's own cryptographic exposure and migration
- Workshop: the authority's quantum readiness action plan

## Structure and Format

Six modules of about three hours each, delivered in person, virtually or in a hybrid format, either as a two-day programme or as a series of half-day sessions. Each module combines a plain-language technical briefing with case studies and supervisory question exercises, and modules can be selected or tailored to the authority's needs.

## Useful Prior Knowledge and Experience

Some experience of cyber, technology risk or operational resilience supervision is helpful. No background in mathematics or cryptography is required.

## Core Reading

- NIST, FIPS 203, 204 and 205: Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards (2024)
- G7 Cyber Expert Group, Public Statement on Planning for the Opportunities and Risks of Quantum Computing (2024)
- NIST, Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 (2024)
